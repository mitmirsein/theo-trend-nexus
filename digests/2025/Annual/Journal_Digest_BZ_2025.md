### Westwerk oder Tempelumbauung Ezechiels binjān Ez 411215b
- **DOI**: [10.30965/25890468-06901001](https://doi.org/10.30965/25890468-06901001)
- **Analytical Tags**: `- / - / -`
- **Abstract**: <jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
<jats:p>In his vision of a future temple (cf. Ezek. 40–42), the prophet Ezekiel mentions, among other things, a structure to which he simply applies the term <jats:italic>binjān</jats:italic> (building, edifice) (cf. Ezek. 41:12–15b). It is generally assumed to be a massive building dominating the western part of the temple area. This article presents a different interpretation, arguing that the term <jats:italic>binjān</jats:italic> refers to the chambers, walls and open spaces mentioned in Ezek. 41,5–11, that surround the actual temple house.</jats:p>

### Maleachi ein Ausleger des Hosea Hosea und Maleachi als literarischer Rahmen des Zwölfprophetenbuches
- **DOI**: [10.30965/25890468-06901002](https://doi.org/10.30965/25890468-06901002)
- **Analytical Tags**: `OT / - / -`
- **Abstract**: <jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
<jats:p>In theories that argue for an original, coherent corpus of the Twelve Minor Prophets, the book of Hosea plays an ambiguous role. While some studies view Hosea as a relative latecomer in the collection, others consider it as an introduction to the Twelve. After briefly presenting the main literary links proposed in the scholarly literature to Malachi, this paper reevaluates them and argues that Hosea and Malachi were shaped as a literary frame around the Twelve. In this redactional process, mainly two passages of Malachi (2:11–16; 3:19–20 MT) were inspired and consciously edited based on Hosea. Malachi employs themes, catchwords, and motifs from Hosea in these passages to adeptly address the theological issues initially raised by Hosea and further expounded upon in other works within the Twelve, ultimately arriving at a satisfying conclusion.</jats:p>

### The Sublimity of the Language of Jesus in the Gospel of John a Comparison with De sublimitate
- **DOI**: [10.30965/25890468-06901003](https://doi.org/10.30965/25890468-06901003)
- **Analytical Tags**: `NT;Systematic Theology / - / -`
- **Abstract**: <jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
<jats:p>This article employs Longinus’ concept of the sublime to interpret and explain aspects of the sublime in the characterisation of Jesus’ language as found in the Fourth Gospel. It begins by observing that two things – the elusiveness of Jesus’ language and Jesus’ presentation as being above ‘earthly’ speakers (John 3:31, 34) – suggest that Jesus’ language is sublime in the rhetorical sense that we find in Longinus’ <jats:italic>De sublimitate</jats:italic>. To test this hypothesis, the second part of the article examines eight key characteristics of the sublime in Longinus’ treatise and compares them with how the language of the Johannine Jesus is depicted. In general, the article shows how John employs these characteristics and argues that John’s adaptation of the notion of the sublime is essential to his Christology and soteriology.</jats:p>

### Petrus die Reinigung der Herzen und die Inklusion in das Volk Gottes Apg 159
- **DOI**: [10.30965/25890468-06901004](https://doi.org/10.30965/25890468-06901004)
- **Analytical Tags**: `NT / - / -`
- **Abstract**: <jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
<jats:p>In his speech at the assembly of the apostles in Jerusalem (Acts 15:6–21), Peter argues for accepting the non-Jewish Christ believers (the <jats:styled-content xml:lang="el-Grek">ἔθνη</jats:styled-content>) as full members of God’s people. One of his statements (Acts 15,9) contains the terms <jats:styled-content xml:lang="el-Grek">καθαρίζω, καρδία</jats:styled-content> and <jats:styled-content xml:lang="el-Grek">πίστις</jats:styled-content>, resulting in an expression quite unusual for Luke. <jats:p>The present article sheds light on the motif of the purification of hearts expressed in Acts 15:9. In doing so, it raises the following questions: Is the idea expressed in Acts 15:9 compatible with these concepts, and is there a further dimension the motif of the purification of hearts unfolds compared to these models? ### Canaan in Genesis 92527 Servant of Brothers Servant of God
- **DOI**: [10.30965/25890468-06901006](https://doi.org/10.30965/25890468-06901006)
- **Analytical Tags**: `OT / - / -`
- **Abstract**: <jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
<jats:p>The final clauses in Genesis 9,26–27 are often seen as continuing the curse on Canaan from verse 25. However, I will argue that verses 26–27 are Noah’s final blessing before his death, rather than part of the curse for Ham’s sin and the antecedent to the pronoun in the final clauses of verses 26–27 is God. So, Canaan is a servant of his brothers in verse 25 but he is a servant of God not of Shem or Japheth in the blessing formula (V. 26–27). I will show that verse 26 is focused upon blessing, identifying, and serving Yhwh while verse 27 shows the relationship between each individual and God (enlarging Japheth, dwelling with Shem, and served by Canaan).</jats:p>

### Ambiguity in Hezekiahs Last Words in Isaiah 398
- **DOI**: [10.30965/25890468-06902001](https://doi.org/10.30965/25890468-06902001)
- **Analytical Tags**: `OT / - / -`
- **Abstract**: <jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
<jats:p>In this article, we argue that the author of Isaiah 39:8b wrote Hezekiah’s final speech, “there will be peace and truth in my days”, in an ambiguous manner. The effect of this ambiguity is doubt in the reader’s mind about Hezekiah. Not only is Hezekiah’s piety questionable, but the syntactical relationship of his speech to the narrative is ambiguous. We show this first by a discussion of the syntax of the parallel passage in 2 Kings 20:19. This is followed by a discussion of the function of initial <jats:styled-content xml:lang="he-Hebr">כי</jats:styled-content> clauses in reported speech within the Hebrew Bible to identify the common categories of usage. Then Isaiah 39:8b is discussed in relation to these categorizations. Finally, Isaiah 39:8 is compared to Isaiah 38:22 to show how Hezekiah’s speech ends both narratives with unclear syntactical and theological connections to the speeches immediately preceding them.</jats:p>

### Der Knecht als Bild Gottes
- **DOI**: [10.30965/25890468-06902002](https://doi.org/10.30965/25890468-06902002)
- **Analytical Tags**: `OT / - / -`
- **Abstract**: <jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
<jats:p>This article deals with the <jats:italic>theology</jats:italic> of the so-called Servant Songs in Isaiah 41–53. As is well known, the question of who the servant of God is, or who he represents, has been discussed at length throughout the centuries. However, the question is usually considered in isolation, in the sense that it is asked and answered independently of who the God associated with the servant is. On the basis of detailed exegesis of concerning texts, this article shows that the appearance and actions of YHWH and the servant are very closely related, that their roles are intertwined. The thesis of the article is that YHWH himself – how and who he is – is at stake in his Èvèd.</jats:p>

<jats:p>To understand the seemingly brutal intervention of God in the story of ANo abstract availableias and Sapphira in Acts 5:1–11, the article classifies it in terms of Formgeschichte as a <jats:italic>narrative about sacrilege and capital punishment</jats:italic>. ### לך Second Person Feminine Singular or Second Person Masculine Singular The Study of Tiberian Prosodic Hierarchy
- **DOI**: [10.30965/25890468-06902005](https://doi.org/10.30965/25890468-06902005)
- **Analytical Tags**: `- / - / -`
- **Abstract**: <jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
<jats:p>Textbooks on Biblical Hebrew grammar, including works by Futato (2003), Verbruggen (2014), and Schneider (2016), identify ‘<jats:styled-content xml:lang="he-Hebr">לָךְ</jats:styled-content>’ as the suffix of the second person feminine singular form. In addition, Bible parsing tools – such as biblehub.com and Bibleworks 10 – also favor ‘<jats:styled-content xml:lang="he-Hebr">לָךְ</jats:styled-content>’ as the suffix of the second person feminine singular. For instance, these Bible parsing tools analyze ‘<jats:styled-content xml:lang="he-Hebr">לָךְ</jats:styled-content>’ as the suffix of the second person feminine singular in the contexts of 1 Samuel 10:7. This article, however, challenges the simple identification in various Biblical Hebrew grammar textbooks, as well as the analyses found on Bible parsing tools. This research posits that the Hebrew preposition ‘<jats:styled-content xml:lang="he-Hebr">לָךְ</jats:styled-content>’ can be identified in two distinct ways: as the suffix of the second person feminine singular form and as the second person masculine singular form. The methodology employed in this study is based on the Tiberian prosodic hierarchy and contextual analysis of the book of Obadiah to prove its thesis.</jats:p>

### A Mouthy Psalm the Poetics of MT Psalm 50 LXX Psalm 49
- **DOI**: [10.30965/25890468-06902006](https://doi.org/10.30965/25890468-06902006)
- **Analytical Tags**: `- / - / -`
- **Abstract**: <jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
<jats:p>The present article observes that Psalm 50 foregrounds mouth activities of speaking and eating. More than that, logics of proximity and possession drive the psalm’s rhetoric, and the mouth is the center of both. Speech closes a spatial gap, and the mouth is the arterial. God is up high, and his speech moves downward to humans; rightful human speech moves upward to God. But animals are already proximate to God on the heights, so the divine mouth is closed to them. The mouth is the arterial for possession as well: God possesses both humans and animals, and on that basis, God speaks to the former, and God declines to consume the latter. Wicked humans are illicit possessors bonded by common eating. The article explores the psalm’s mouthiness specifically in the context of translation. It toggles between MT Ps 50 and LXX Ps 49 to test the significance of verbal form to the poetics of the psalm.</jats:p>
