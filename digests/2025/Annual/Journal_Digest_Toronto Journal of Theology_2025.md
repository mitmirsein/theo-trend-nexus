### Artificial Intelligence and the Question of God in Evolution
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2024-0037
- **Analytical Tags**: `OT / nan / nan`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p> This article examines the integral relationship between artificial intelligence (AI) and religion with a particular focus on the question of God. By viewing AI as a natural development of human evolution, I examine the way AI has been co-opted by technology as a replacement for religion. Teilhard de Chardin saw the import of computer technology for the further evolution of humankind; however, his ideas require a radicalization of theology, along the lines of natural philosophy. The weak theology of John Caputo is helpful to revisit Teilhard's understanding of God as a power or presence in evolution. In this respect, his ideas are similar to FWJ Schelling's notion of theogenesis. Understanding Teilhard within a post-theist framework allows a greater appreciation of his support of computer technology as a new level of theogenesis marked by a new unity of planetary life. The development of AI can be framed within a broader evolutionary process that carries profound spiritual implications and might be understood as an expression of divine becoming, propelling humanity towards a more integrated and holistic existence. </jats:p>

### Man Versus Machine Grappling with Analogy
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2024-0053
- **Analytical Tags**: `nan / nan / nan`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p> There have been some remarkable advances made with machine learning in recent years. The abilities of artificial intelligence (AI) technology are often evaluated using the Turing test, which leads to terminology normally applied to human beings being applied to AI. After a brief introduction to machine learning, this paper notes that this approach to evaluating AI is analogical and argues that the analogy is not a stable one. The most obvious and used analogy is with the human mind, which itself presents an unresolved problem in philosophy. When the details of some of the common brain-mind theories are examined, they tend to undermine the analogical argument in a way that parallels some of John Searle's concerns. </jats:p>

### Which Limits of Virtual Religious Life On the Experience of Holy in the Digital Age
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2024-0058
- **Analytical Tags**: `nan / nan / Contemporary`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p> In this article the possibility of an online Eucharist is assessed as a special case of virtual religious life and of a digital experience of the holy. More precisely, two types of arguments are given against the possibility of an online Eucharist—of a theological and of a phenomenological nature. While the theological argument highlights the shared and receptive dimension of the Eucharist, which makes possible its intrinsically communal and incarnated nature, the phenomenological argument shows that this receptive and shared dimension presupposes the materiality of the host, which in its turn makes possible the experience of its holiness, namely as that which cannot be profaned through bodily actions. Here Otto's and Eliade's opposition between the holy and the profane is revisited, whereby the holy is conceived not merely as the opposite of the profane, but as that which cannot be profaned, and as that which implies the prohibition of particular actions and bodily gestures that can be carried out only on a material object. Hence, materiality and holiness are intrinsically correlated following this understanding of the holy. Drawing on this reinterpretation of the concept of holy, it is argued that virtuality cannot preserve the materiality and consequently the holy dimension of the host, and for this reason, that it cannot preserve the receptive and shared dimension of the Eucharist. Hence, despite advances in digital technology, the Eucharist appears to be an insuperable limit of virtual religious life, at least in our contemporary world and its current digital possibilities. </jats:p>

### How Eberhard Arnold Interpreted Nietzsche
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2024-0061
- **Analytical Tags**: `NT / nan / Contemporary`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p> Eberhard Arnold, the founder of the Bruderhof communities, often engaged with Friedrich Nietzsche's philosophy, offering both defensive and constructive responses. This article explores some of the most noteworthy ways that Arnold interacted with Nietzsche's ideas. While aligning with Nietzsche's critique of Christianity's shift toward afterlife obsession, Arnold advocated a return to early Christianity's celebration of this life. Arnold's theology centered on hope and fulfillment, challenging conventional morality and law-like religion, and underscoring the importance of the inner life, while refusing to pit it against the material world. The article also highlights several productive themes that arise from the intersection of Arnold and Nietzsche by recruiting historical and contemporary interlocutors to make sense of the culmination of Arnold's response: establishing Bruderhof communities as living embodiments of his convictions, transcending rational answers, and exemplifying a life-affirming Christian witness. </jats:p>

### With Orca Goose and Bear Expanding Canadas Ritual Body
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2024-0066
- **Analytical Tags**: `Practical Theology;Systematic Theology / nan / nan`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p>The publication of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada in 2015 was a significant moment in both the story of Canadian-Indigenous relations and church-Indigenous relations. Churches across Canada have been wrestling not only with their complicity in the operation of residential schools but with their wider role in European colonialism. Many of the churches have initiated significant strategies for learning and education, and have explored ritual responses to residential schools and colonialism, such as land acknowledgements, art installations, and the reading of parts of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada. This paper is not quite about these ritual practices. Instead, it takes a wider lens, asking about the context in which these practices take place: what does it mean to “do” liturgy—to practice Christian ritual, to ritualize—in Canada today? More broadly, however, it asks how settler colonialism—particular practices of violence and domination—impact ritualization in contexts across the world—and how this is all rooted in the land. This paper proceeds in three parts. First, it discusses the concepts of the body, ritualization, and contextualization in Catherine Bell's Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice. Second, it examines settler colonialism using my own context of Canada as a particular example. Third, it draws on Cláudio Carvalhaes’ concept of lex naturae to expand Bell's concept of the “body”: all (liturgical) practice must be seen through the lens of creation. Through a brief case study, it then demonstrates one way in which a Christian community has attempted to bring the voice of the land into liturgical practice and thus expand the ritualization of “bodies” in Christian ritual. Because of the way that settler colonialism functions in its global contexts, ritualization should understand the “body” as including the land and the interrelationship of all created things.</jats:p>

### Science and Religion New Avenues Beyond the Fixed Categories
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2024-0069
- **Analytical Tags**: `nan / nan / nan`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p> Building on recent work by historians of science, many scholars studying the relationships between religion, theology, and science have stopped treating the concepts of science and religion as if they were natural kinds. But if we refrain from using these terms in an essentialist way, what comes next? Should both categories be abandoned, or perhaps replaced by other ones? This article argues that (1) in some cases the use of science and religion as generalizing concepts remains both unavoidable and responsible, (2) in other cases we need more specificity and acknowledgement of their contextuality, and (3) in general we need more attentiveness to newly emerging configurations of the ever-changing relationships between science(s) and religion(s). In particular, it is suggested that in the near future we may see science and religion coming together in an unprecedented way, namely as allies in a shared struggle against post-truth thinking, institution-skepticism, and societal marginalization. </jats:p>

### Reading Us and Our in Genesis 126 in Light of Al-Qurān
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2024-0071
- **Analytical Tags**: `OT;Systematic Theology / nan / nan`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p>In Genesis 1:26, the term “Us” and “Our” have been subject to various interpretations within Christian theology. One prevalent interpretation posits that “Us” and “Our” signify the doctrine of the Trinity, encompassing God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Spirit. This perspective recognizes the presence of the Trinitarian concept—one essence manifested in three distinct persons—within the creation narrative. Conversely, another interpretation challenges the notion that “Us” and “Our” pertain to the Trinity, suggesting instead that it refers to God with his angels or a heavenly assembly. The last interpretation proposes that the plural verb signifies the majestic plural. This study, however, introduces an alternative interpretation, proposing that “Us” and “Our” communicates both the divine attributions of God and human responsibilities. The article employs a comparative literature methodology to analyze the creation narratives found in both the Holy Bible and the Al-Qur'ān.</jats:p>

### Reimagining 1819 Congo Square Affect Rhythm and the Disruption of Racialized Power
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2024-0072
- **Analytical Tags**: `NT / nan / nan`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p> This article examines Benjamin Latrobe's 1819 account of Congo Square in New Orleans to explore the intersections of sound, race, and power through the lens of affect theory. Latrobe's dismissal of the drumming, dancing, and singing as mere “noise” reflects the racialized epistemologies that framed Black cultural practices as chaotic and subhuman. Using theoretical frameworks from Donovan Schaefer, Yvonne Daniel, and Antonio Benítez-Rojo, the article interrogates how sound and embodied practices disrupt hegemonic sensory and social landscapes. Part 1 analyzes Latrobe's affective response, illustrating how his sensory discomfort exposed the limits of whiteness and its reliance on the dehumanization of Blackness. Part 2 situates Congo Square within the broader Afro-diasporic world, drawing on Daniel's concept of “embodied mathematics” and Benítez-Rojo's “repetition with difference” to highlight the epistemological and spiritual dimensions of these performances. These practices, far from chaotic, were acts of resistance and cultural sovereignty, asserting Black subjectivity in the face of systemic erasure. By reframing Congo Square as a site of “otherwise possibilities,” this article emphasizes the generative potential of Afro-diasporic traditions to imagine alternative modes of social organization and mobilization. It also positions New Orleans at the nexus of American religious and cultural history, inviting a reevaluation of migration, Afro-Caribbean religion, and Francophone culture as foundational to the American experience. Ultimately, Congo Square's rhythms reveal the enduring power of sound and movement to challenge domination and envision new futures. </jats:p>

### Life with Cows and Trinity A Reflection on Community Choice and Authenticity in an Individualized Ag
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2025-0042
- **Analytical Tags**: `Systematic Theology / nan / nan`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p>This reflection explores the interplay between community, choice, and authenticity in an age shaped by individualism. Beginning with a memory of the way in which a community once kept a herd of cows, it examines how their disappearance symbolizes the erosion of shared practices and the rise of the “buffered self,” as articulated by Charles Taylor. Through Taylor's framework of secularity and authenticity, and theological insights, particularly from Miroslav Volf, the reflection suggests that true identity and formation arise not in isolation but through communal participation. The cows, once central to a rhythm of shared life, represent a formative practice lost to the prioritization of individual autonomy. Through this reflection, the authentic life is shown to be found in community and communion with others, creation, and God.</jats:p>

### Kierkegaard and Eliade on Repetition
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2025-0044
- **Analytical Tags**: `nan / nan / nan`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p>The concept of repetition is one of the most recurrent themes covering both Eliade's scientific work and his Fantastic Tales. Given that Søren Kierkegaard was among the authors most read by Eliade, it is important to recognize that Eliade owed much to the concept of repetition originally developed by Kierkegaard. Given that, this article aims to speculate about how the Kierkegaardian concept of repetition could have affected Eliade's way of interpreting the history of religion and how it could have been a source of inspiration for him as an author of fantasy fiction. At the very beginning, the article aims to show the extent of Eliade's knowledge of Kierkegaard by briefly retracing Kierkegaard's impact on Eliade. Then it clarifies what Kierkegaard means by repetition and how this concept can be recognized as at work in Eliade as a historian of religions. With this regard, the article emphasizes two points: Eliade held firm the distinction between archaic and modern religious attitudes on the basis of the Kierkegaardian category of “the moment” as “the fullness of time,” and Eliade found in Kierkegaard the original input to think both the “unrecognizability of the miracle” and the “camouflage of the sacred.” Finally, the article aims to show the relevance of Kierkegaard's concept of repetition on Eliade's Fantastic Tales by examining a couple of them: “The Bridge” (1963) and “Incognito in Buchenwald” (1974).</jats:p>

### Uncertainty Flourishing and Existential Psychotherapy Referencing Muhammad Iqbals Model of Self
- **DOI**: 10.3138/tjt-2025-0052
- **Analytical Tags**: `nan / nan / nan`
- **Abstract**: <jats:p>This paper discusses an alternative way of thinking about self as a psychosocial dynamic process advanced by Muhammad Iqbal (1876–1935), a cosmopolitan philosopher-psychologist of Indo-Islamic heritage. His model of self is advanced here as a resource for an existential psychotherapy approach more hospitable for working with mental health clients of an Islamic cultural background than approaches informed by the existential thinking strand in a European philosophical tradition. Iqbal's model of the self is open to an understanding of truth and uncertainty as factors in how we live and think, and how we find or situate ourselves in the world. The sketch here of his model of self shows recognition that uncertainty is a necessary ingredient for health and wellbeing, and not merely an obstacle in self-identity forming.</jats:p>
